States that the force $F=k\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$, where $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the magnitudes of the point charges that are separated by a distance $r$ in a vacuum. $k$ is Coulomb's constant.

The alternative way of expressing the equation is by replacing $k$ with $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$;
$$
	\begin{aligned}
	F &= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\times \frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}=\frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}
	\end{aligned}
$$
Constant $\varepsilon_0$ is known as the **permittivity of free space** ('free space' means a vacuum).

The equation for Coulomb's law, above, only applies when the charges are in **a vacuum** (or in air, which has a permittivity $\approx$ that of a vacuum).

When charges are in a medium other than a vacuum or air, $\varepsilon_0$ must be replaced by $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$ where $\varepsilon_r$ is the **relative permittivity** of the medium. Hence, Coulomb's law would be:
$$
	F=\frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rr^2}
$$
**Relative permittivity** is a measure of how easily the molecules that make up the substance can be rotated in an electric field. Relative permittivity has no units as it is a ratio.


Coulomb's law can predict the **direction of the electric force**; when both $q_1$ and $q_2$ are positive, the force $F$ is also positive. The force must **repel** in such a situation.

Hence, if (at most) one of the two charges is negative, $F$ is negative; the law predicts that the charges are **attracted**.
